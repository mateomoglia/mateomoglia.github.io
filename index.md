<h2 class="section-title">Job Market Paper</h2>

<div class="paper-head">
  <p class="paper-title">The Hidden Costs of Urban Renewal</p>
  <a class="pill" href="https://mateomoglia.github.io/files/JMPMoglia.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PDF</a>
</div>
<button class="abstract-btn" onclick="toggleAbstract(this)">
  <span class="arr">&#9654;</span> abstract
</button>
<div class="abstract-body">
This paper studies three wedges that hinder urban redevelopment, (i) market structure, as land is bought by concentrated developers, (ii) market frictions, through rent uncertainty, high fixed costs, and time to build, and (iii) market regulation, through taxes and land-use regulations. I formalise them in an option value model in which landowners sell parcels to oligopsonistic developers. Developers mark down the price of land and this market failure delays only the parcels that carry a building. A first-best policy requires that the government observe the composition of fixed costs parcel by parcel and control the whole permitting process. Neither condition holds in practice: I therefore study corrective, second-best, and rent-minimising policies. I calibrate the model on 600,000 parcels in the Paris metropolitan area and recover a parcel-specific fixed cost net of the other wedges. A tax on the standing value of under-used parcels, at a rate set by local developer concentration, restores efficiency, raises revenue, lowers rents by 0.50%, and brings the redevelopment of the next twenty years forward by 1.9 years. In contrast, the best rent-minimising mix, within legal bounds and under a balanced budget, cuts the taxes on new construction and taxes land instead. It lowers market rents by 14.02% over twenty years, mostly through a transfer from incumbent landlords to renters, and brings redevelopment forward by 14.6 years.
</div>

<h2 class="section-title">Working Papers</h2>

<ul class="paper-list">

  <li class="paper-item">
    <div class="paper-head">
      <p class="paper-title">The Spatial Impacts of Population Ageing</p>
      <a class="pill" href="https://mateomoglia.github.io/files/SpatialImpactsAgeing.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PDF</a>
    </div>
    <button class="abstract-btn" onclick="toggleAbstract(this)">
      <span class="arr">&#9654;</span> abstract
    </button>
    <div class="abstract-body">
      Rich countries are ageing and will all soon lose population. This paper examines the impact of this major demographic shift on welfare and inequality by taking into account its wide spatial heterogeneity. Using administrative data on population, housing markets, and local public finance in France, I reveal a new trade-off related to population ageing: an increase in housing costs and in local tax revenues. I build and calibrate a quantitative spatial model in which households differ in terms of skill, age, and family size. I then use this model to run counterfactual experiments based on demographic forecasts. The results show small positive impacts on welfare albeit with an increase in spatial inequalities.
    </div>
    <p class="presentations">Presentations: 2025 Association Française des Economistes de la Population (Marseille), CREST Internal seminar, 2025 RIEF Doctoral Meeting (Marseille), 2025 Doctorissimes (PSE), 2025 European Economic Association Meeting (Bordeaux), 2025 Conference on Regional Economic Inequalities (Berlin)</p>
  </li>

  <li class="paper-item">
    <div class="paper-head">
      <p class="paper-title">Age-Specific Preferences for Climate Amenities</p>
      <a class="pill" href="https://mateomoglia.github.io/files/AgeClimatePreferences.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PDF</a>
    </div>
    <p class="paper-meta">With <a href="https://briceberland.github.io" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Brice Berland</a></p>
    <button class="abstract-btn" onclick="toggleAbstract(this)">
      <span class="arr">&#9654;</span> abstract
    </button>
    <div class="abstract-body">
      How do individuals value climate as a residential amenity over the life cycle? How does climate change affect the spatial allocation of climate amenities? Using individual-level census data and high-resolution climate projections for France, we document robust age heterogeneity in climate sorting: older cohorts move less often than prime-age workers, but when they do, they systematically choose thermally more comfortable destinations. We estimate age-specific marginal willingnesses to pay (MWTP) for climate amenities in a framework à la Bayer et al. (2009) and embed these preferences in a quantitative spatial model to simulate the general-equilibrium effects of climate change on population distribution and local markets until 2100. Older cohorts rarely relocate: those in warming regions bear most of the welfare costs, while those in improving regions gain. Younger, more mobile cohorts adapt by migrating toward better climates. The resulting shifts in the age composition of local areas matter for adaptation policy and for public good provision.\
    </div>
    <p class="presentations">Presentations (incl. by co-author&#42;): 2026 Association Française des Economistes de la Population&#42; (Strasbourg), PhD Workshop in Urban Econmics (HEC), CREST PhD Seminar&#42;</p>
  </li>
